What the final test established about automatic filing and human review

Automatic filing

On the -page final test, of pages qualified for automatic filing at the confidence cutoff, which was frozen before testing. Of those pages, were assigned to the correct department, below the target, although the 95% interval (–) includes it. Departments whose records state their purpose plainly scored highest: Marketing and Research (F1 and ).

Human review

The remaining pages were routed for review with a retrieval-augmented suggestion. On these pages, the suggestion named the correct department of the time, compared with for the classifier alone, and the correct department was among its first two choices of the time. Review concentrated on interpretive boundaries, above all Corporate affairs against Legal (F1 , the lowest), thin first pages and handwriting; all unusable test pages reached a person.

I deliberately evaluated LocalRIM on scanned paper records: typewritten pages, handwritten annotations and degraded copies. Reading quality varied by scan group, and better text was associated with higher classification scores in cross-validation ( to points of macro F1). These results show that document quality matters; they do not establish how the pipeline would perform on newer or born-digital records.

How the findings changed my approach

Several decisions in LocalRIM came from testing assumptions that initially seemed reasonable.

From a general taxonomy to a client-specific one

I began with a general taxonomy and tested it on a public benchmark containing documents from several companies. The approach proved unsuitable because each company organized its records differently, making it difficult to apply one set of categories consistently. I therefore focused the project on Philip Morris as the client, using its retention schedules and records-management documents to develop the final taxonomy. Between the two pilots, labels marked uncertain fell from to , although the labeling application was revised during the same period, so the reduction cannot be attributed to the taxonomy alone.

Testing orientation instead of accepting the model's recommendation

PaddleOCR's orientation model proposed rotating pages, but only recommendations were correct; the other pages were already upright. I retained the approach established in D-30: the pipeline reads flagged pages in both orientations and keeps the output with higher OCR confidence. This prevented an unverified orientation recommendation from automatically changing the text used downstream.

Keeping Bates numbers without letting the classifier rely on them

Bates numbers help identify and retrieve records, but a model using only their first four digits achieved macro F1 when predicting departments. Because litigation stamps were applied box by box, the identifier could provide a shortcut unrelated to a document's purpose. I retained Bates numbers in OCR output but excluded them from classifier input (D-46). Cross-validated macro F1 was with the identifiers and without them; removing them did not reduce the observed score.

What these decisions established

The central lesson was that filing depends on understanding how the client organizes and uses its records. A CV may belong to Human Resources or to Research and Development depending on why it was kept; the taxonomy pilots made that concrete. The categories had to reflect the client's records practice, not just the subjects a document mentions.

An employee's CV→Human Resources
A CV inside a grant application→Research and Development

Labeling turned this into a written rule (R3).

I also fixed the evaluation rules before the final test: the selected pipeline, the confidence cutoff and the routing conditions. The test pages were labeled without seeing any model prediction, and the frozen pipeline was scored once. That is why the final results can be reported as they came out, without adjusting the system to improve them.

  • No study of reviewers. Suggestions were scored against my labels, not tested on whether reviewers became more accurate, faster or more confident when using them.
  • The minimum-text rule needs more testing. On the final test, no page under 20 tokens was filed automatically, but filed pages had 60 tokens or fewer, and the -token one went to the wrong department. A 20-token minimum does not guarantee enough text to classify a page.
