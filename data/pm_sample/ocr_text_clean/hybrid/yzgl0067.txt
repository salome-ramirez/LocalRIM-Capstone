Nicotine Availability in Tobacco SmokeEnhanced
By Ammonia
The amount of nicotine available to a smoker from a
tobacco source can be adjusted by varying the levels
of ammonia in the smoke, according to Dr. James F.
Pankow of the Oregon Graduate Institute of Science
and Technology in Portland. He has found that the
addition of ammonia to tobacco smoke particles from
commercially available cigarettes greatly increases the
volatility of the nicotine from those particles. This
effect has been cited by the U.S. Food and Drug
Administration, and has not, up to now, been
demonstrated by any study conducted in the public
domain. Pankow's research appears in the August
issue of the peer-reviewed journal Environmental
Science & Technology, a publication of the American
Chemical Society.
Although Pankow has shown that ammonia does
make nicotine more available from cigarette smoke,
he cautions that work needs to be done to determine
whether "the increased chemical availability translates
into a more rapid uptake of nicotine by the smoker."
The monoprotonated form, in which a hydrogen ion
has been attached to one of the nitrogen atoms in the
nicotine molecule, is present in the liquid tobacco
smoke particles, but does not readily pass into the
gaseous phase of cigarette smoke. In the presence of
ammonia, the hydrogen ion is removed from the
monoprotonated form, leaving the nicotine molecule
in its unprotonated, or free-base form. It is in this form
that nicotine can more easily vaporize from the smoke
particles into the gas phase, where it can deposit
directly to the lung tissue and begin diffusing into the
body immediately, Pankow says.
