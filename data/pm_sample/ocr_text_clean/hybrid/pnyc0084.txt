Attachment 17
IN VITRO RECONSTITUTED TISSUE AS AN ALTERNATIVE TO HUMAN
RESPIRATORY TRACT
Emura, M.1, A. Ochiai2 and S. Hirohashi2
1Hannover Medical School, Hannover, Germany and 2National Cancer Center, Tokyo,
Japan
The data obtained from in vitro systems utilizing human cells and tissues should form a
basic part of the information necessary for risk assessment and legislation. The most
important thing for such systems is, therefore, to sinulate the structures and functions
of cells and tissues in the natíve organ as closely as possible. This is a fundamental
requirement that has only been met occasionally among the in vitro systems published
so far and it appears to provide a difficult task for the people working to improve the
systems.
Nevertheless, every effort to attain this goal seems justified, particularly in view of the
prevalent lack of adequate knowledge to determine the predictability of the data
obtained from the in vitro systems currently available.
In designing in vitro systems, there may be two approaches - one aiming at the growth
of cells in a primarily two-dimensional fashion, the other allowing cells to form in vivo
- mimicking three-dimensional architectures. Which of these different growth patterns
will be expressed in culture is subject to the choice of substrata, to which the cells
assume contact. In addition, the type of contact, namely one-sided, partial, pointed,
whole-surface, etc. will strongly influence the growth pattem. The migratory capacity
of the cells can be an important factor, which may be modified (limited or enhanced)
by the physicochemical nature of the contact substrata. The original architecture of the
native tissue from which the cells have derived should also be taken into account.
In cultures, in which the airway epithelial cells are growing in a two-dimensional
fashion, some functional and structural characteristics, such as Clara cell antigenicity,
production of surfactant phosphatidylcholine or activity of polycyclic aromatic
hydrocarbon metabolizing enzymes can be developed to a considerable extent.
However, there are some that cannot be developed or expressed under that condition
but require a three-dimensional growth pattern, for instance ciliogenesis and mucus
production.
In this paper we will explore the capacity of early to long-term passage airway
epithelial cells (human and hamster) to resume architectures and functions existing in
the native tissue in the specific environments given in vitro.
