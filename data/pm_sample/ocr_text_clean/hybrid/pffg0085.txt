HOW GOOD ARE THE NUMBERS FOR CIGARETTE
TAR AT PREDICTING DELIVERIES OF CARBON
MONOXIDE, HYDROGEN CYANIDE, AND ACROLEIN?
J. C. Young, J. C. Robinson, W. S. Rickert
Department of Statistics, University of Waterloo,
Waterloo, Ontario, N2L 3G1 Canada and Labstat
Incorporated, Kitchener, Ontario, N2C 1L3 Canada
Simple and multiple linear regression analyses were performed on the tar,
nicotine, CO, HCN, and acrolein deliveries of 98 brands of commercially available
Cunadian cigarettes. Simple linear regressions of CO, HCN, and acrolein on tar and of
acrolein on CO were not significant at the 5% level for plain cigarettes; all ather
regressions were very strongly significunt (p < 0.O1). In addition there was strong
statistical evidence that filter, vented filter, and plain cigarettes have different
regression relations and different levels of precision associated with the regression
purameters. With respect to CO, tar level could predict the corresponding CO value
only within 3.9 mg (filter), 1.9 mg (vented filter), and 3.1 mg (plain). These results
show that tar delivery alone is an inadequate predictor of gas-phase constituents such
as CO. Information concerning these constituents is best conveyed by an analytically
determined value for each component.
INTRODUCTION
Since 1954, when the first reports appeared linking cigarette smoking
to lung cancer, cigarette tar deliveries have steadily declined, resulting in a
measurable decrease in excess mortality for smokers who switched to
low-tar brands (Hammond et al., 1976). With respect to specific diseases,
reductions in mortality rates appear to be greatest for lung cancer, which
is not surprising since cigarette tar contains at least 40 carcinogens along
with various tumor initiators and promoters (Hoffmann et al., 1976).
However, reductions in the rate of coronary heart disease have been less
dramatic, prompting the suggestion that a gaseous constituent of tobacco
smoke, such as CO, may be important in the development of this disease
(Wald, 1976). Indeed, CO was identified in the 1979 report of the U.S.
Surgeon General as a possible critical factor in a number of conditions
including coronary heart disease, atherosclerosis and sudden death, chronic (TITLE 17 U.S. CODE)
