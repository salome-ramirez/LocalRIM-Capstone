"Tax Features"
And Tobacco
Washington has seen some pretty strange
sights in recent years, and so it reaily
takes an effort to surprise. But President
Clinton has come through again. His own
Treasury Department projects a cumula-
tive surplus of $714 billion over the next
five years and yet the President proposes
a net tax hike of $45 billion in his budget.
To put his tax increase in context,
the Office of Management and Budget
projects federal taxes to reach 20.7 per-
cent of GDP in 1999,a modern record.
Only at the very peak of World War II
spending in 1944 did this ratio reach a
higher level of 20.9 percent. Even more
relevant is the tax burden per capita. The
Tax Foundation estimated the federal per
capita tax burden to be $6,810 in 1998
with a sizable increase expected in 1999.
When President Clinton took office, the
per capita federal tax burden was $5,441
when adjusted for inflation. That’s a
$1,368 increase, or over 25 percent.
Curiously missing from the
President's voluminous budget submis-
sion is the distribution of the President's
net tax hike by income class. Tax Founda-
tion senior economist Patrick Fleenor
rectified the omission by publishing it in
Number 84 of the Foundation's Special
Report series, The President’s Fiscal Year
2000 Budget. The table displays the
President's second surprise — his plan is
highly regressive. Taxpayers with incomes
below $50,000 in adjusted gross income
would pay over 60 percent of the net tax
increase, and taxpayers making less than
$25,000 will pay the majority of that, over
38 percent.
The plan is so regressive because its
centerpiece is a proposed 55-cent-per-
pack increase in the tobacco excise.
Recall last year the tobacco companies
agreed to pay the states hundreds of
billions of dollars, supposedly compen-
sating them for costs incurred in their
public health programs due to smoking.

that pool of cash, but the federal deal feli

The settlement's justification was
that smokers cost society extra. A recent
study by Jane Gravelle of the Congres-
sional Research Institute and published
by the Tax Foundation notes that smokers
raised Medicare and Medicaid’s health
care costs by about $9 billion annually.
She also notes, however, that because
smokers tend to die younger they reduce
Social Security outlays by about $40 bil
lion and reduce nursing home costs
borne by Medicare by about $8 billion.
Factor in cigarette tax revenue, and
Gravelle concludes that the federal gov-
ernment saves almost $35 billion each
year due to smoking and the states save
$2.1 billion. These estimates don’t war-
rant subsidizing cigarettes, but they cast
grave doubt on the fiscal excuses offered
up to justify federal and state cigarette
excises. -
That hasn't stopped the states who
are clearly not satisfied with their extraor-
dinary haul from the tobacco companies.
Even knowing that their poorest citizens
will bear the load of any new taxes on
cigarettes, five state governments raised.
their cigarette tax rates in 1998; Hawaii
from 80 cents per pack to $1.00 (tieing
Alaska for the highest rate), New Jersey
from 40 to 80 cents, Illinois from 44 to 58
cents, Pennsylvania from 61 to 71 cents,
and Wisconsin from 44 to 59 cents,
Governor Glendenning of Maryland
has proposed putting his state at the top
of the cigarette tax charts with a $1-per-
pack hike on top of the existing 36-cent
state tax. As a rationalization, he has re-
sorted to another popular argument for
higher cigarette excises, saying that this
would discourage underage smoking. The
governor should not mock such a serious
problem with a proposal designed to fail.
Only two percent of cigarettes are
smoked by the underage, yet he would
have all Maryland smokers—a mostly
low-income group—pay more tax be-
cause he is unwilling to enforce laws
already on the books against underage
smoking. The state could ban cigarettes
from vending machines or crack down on
retailers, but those tactics wouldn't put
more money in the state treasury.
According to the University of
Michigan’s Monitoring the Future Survey,
while 36.5 percent of high school seniors
smoked at least one cigarette in the prior
month, only 24.6 percent smoked at least
one a day, and onfy 14.3 percent smoked
more than ten cigarettes daily. For a teen-
ager smoking ten a day, a $1 per pack tax
hike amounts to $3.50 a week extra cost.
The effect of the price hike is likely to be
biunted by the fact, observed by Gravelle,
that teenagers tend to buy higher priced
premium brands because they smoke for
status, Status smokers are more likely to
be occasional smokers. Thus they can
afford premium brands and, in all likeli-
hood, they will pay the taxes with virtu-
ally no change in their behavior.
However, some people will be forced
to change. A low-income couple that
smokes a pack a day each will be facing
at least $750 more in taxes each year. Will
they give up cigarettes or necessities? You
be the judge. Ü
When examined closely, virtually all
fiscal arguments in favor of imposing any
tobacco excise taxes, let alone higher
taxes, simply fall. The only arguments left
standing are that smoking is unpopular in
important quarters and governments
need only a thin excuse to raise a tax.
Even so, it takes real audacity for
President Clinton to suggest that 2 gov-
emment in surplus, already imposing the
heaviest peacetime tax burden in history,
needs more revenue from lower-income
taxpayers. Perhaps that is the one legacy
of the Administration on which we can all
agree—it never lacked audacity. ®
