10th Conference of the International Society for Environmental Epidemiology and 8th Conference of the International Society of Exposure Analysis

“Connecting with the community in environmental health studies”

Boston, August 15-18, 1998

General remarks:

With approx. 700 registered scientists, this joint conference had the largest attendance ever. Approx. 40% of the attendees were from overseas, 15% from Asia/Pacific, 20% from Europe (West and East). Approx. 50% of the scientists were female.

The key issue/topic (as reflected by the conference title, plenary talks and pre-conference workshop) was ‘community involvement’ in environmental epidemiology. This indicates a major reorientation of the society from ‘pure science’, and ‘academic science’ to ‘integrated science’ and ‘lay science’. Environmental activists invited to present key plenary lectures (G. Latowsky, E. Mann) articulated this development as moving from ‘Corporate science’ to ‘peoples science’, the latter claimed to better represent and publicize the ‘truth’. It is my interpretation that ISEE moves from environmental epidemiology to ‘green’ epidemiology, from science to advocacy, from risk analysis to risk management by embracing the public (non-corporate stakeholders, lay activists, environmentalists groups, public health authorities, media, politicians) in its epidemiological projects. It is based on the opinion frequently expressed by conference speakers that science is not value-free and that ‘academic’ and ‘corporate’ science has not served public health well. This is the perceived trend in a scientific society, where D. Dockery, B. Brunekreef, D. Wartenberg, G. Pershagen, J. Spengler are key officers of the society or organizers of the Boston conference.

The abstracts of the conference contributions are published in Vol. 9(4), July 1998 Supplement of Epidemiology S22-S167. The abstracts cited below are attached to this report.

The session “Assessment of Ambient Air Pollution Concentrations” (co-chaired by Helen Suh and Patrick Kinney) featured reports on field studies on the one side and on improvements of measurement techniques on the other (efficacy of PM2.5 and PM10 deposition on filters, evaluation of weight differences in the microgram range). A.D. Kyle discussed an interesting idea to prevent the hitherto dichotomic assessment of air quality (above or below threshold). He proposed to construct a summarical index of outdoor air quality consisting of the parameters CO, NO2, SO2, PM, O3, and Pb.

The session “Sources of Indoor Particles I” (chair W. Ott) featured an interesting presentation on the importance of house vacuuming as a particle source by A. Ferro (Stanford University, abstract 117 O). This study had been supported by CIAR (acknowledgement given) and found that the changes in PM10 by vacuuming are not dramatic, whereas PM2.5 are increased by one order of magnitude. The subsequent discussion centered on the “personal cloud” of PM2.5 that is created by every individual by its activity and/or the thermoconvection around the body. L. Wallace reported on the PAH concentration in homes. His preferred instrument (supported by others in the discussion) was the real-time monitor PAS 2000 that measured with a yet unknown precision PAH’s attached to the particle surface (abstract 120 O).
