LARGE INCREASES IN TOBACCO TAXES WOULD CAUSE MAJOR
JOB LOSSES
Bills introduced in Congress and proposed in the President's budget, would increase
Federal tobacco excise taxes or tobacco-related fees, by $0.50 to $0.65 per pack of
cigarettes in 1999; $1.23 to $1.59 by 2003; and $1.79 to $1.92 by 2008 (in nominal terms).
The effective excise tax increases actually would be as high as $2.46 in 2003 and $2.84 in
2008 under the Kennedy Bill, due to the added cost of denied deductibility of the tax
increases. Possible “look-back" surcharges could result in even higher fees in the future.
Large increases in tobacco excise taxes or fees would impose large employment
dislocations on the U.S. economy. Many workers will suffer real hardships, and long-
term unemployment or underemployment after they are laid off, even if they are
successful in eventually finding new jobs.
134,000 to 455,000 workers across the U.S. would experience job losses as a result of
enactment of the proposed tobacco tax or fee increases, depending upon which
proposal is ultimately adopted.
Job dislocations would affect every state in the country, directly or indirectly. The job
dislocations would be directly felt in states where tobacco growing, processing or
product manufacturing are major industries, as higher tobacco taxes reduce sales of
tobacco products, which in turn would result in job losses in tobacco-related industries.
Job dislocations also would be felt more generally in the wholesale and retail sectors
and in the wide range of industries that provide goods and services that directly or
indirectly contribute to the manufacture, distribution and sale of tobacco products.
