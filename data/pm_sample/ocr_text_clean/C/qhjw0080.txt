U.S. Cigarette Smuggling Called $1 Billion Problem
By George F.W. Telfer, The Journal of Commerce
Knight-Ridder/Tribune Business News
NEW ORLEANS--Sept. 2--A shipment of U.S.-made cigarettes ostensibly being
exported to Mexico and thus not taxed is loaded aboard a freighter in a
California port or onto a truck in California or Texas.
The cigarettes loaded onto the ship never get to Mexico because they're unloaded
before the ship leaves port and sold untaxed at a huge profit in California or
other states with high taxes on tobacco products.
The cigarettes on the truck, perhaps bought legally at a duty-free shop, actually
are transported to Mexico. There they're either sold directly to smugglers or
transferred to another truck in, say, Tijuana. Then they're driven back across the
border into a designated parking lot in San Diego. After that, they're distributed
to retail outlets in Los Angeles, where they sell for as little as 75 cents a carton.
Such smuggling of cigarettes in the United States has surged in the last three
years as federal and state cigarette taxes rose, mirroring a pattern in Canada a
few years earlier, said Rodney Stamler, a Canadian expert on smuggling of
cigarettes and illicit drugs by organized crime elements.
Tax evasion through such contraband will cost the federal government and six
states with high taxes on tobacco products $1 billion in revenue this year,Stamler
said.
A former assistant commissioner of the Royal Canadian Mounted Police, he now
is a lobbyist for American cigarette makers and marketers who have formed the
National Coalition Against Crime and Tobacco Contraband.
A study released recently by a forensic financial investigating firm that employs
him, Lindquist Avey Macdonald Baskerville of Washington, put this
year's federal cigarette revenue loss at as much as $345 million and the six states'
losses at up to $645 million.
Last year, the cigarette contraband probably cost the federal government and the
six states - California, Illinois, New Jersey, New York, Texas and Washington - as
much as $905 million; in 1992, it mäy have cost them about $350 million, the
study found.
