Does smoking cause lung cancer?

A medical physicist who once accepted the conventional view that lung cancer is almost entirely due to smoking, outlines the evidence that has forced him to change his mind.

Professor Philip Burch
has a personal chair in the department of medical physics, University of Leeds

In their 1971 report, the Royal College of Physicians stressed: "Many countries have set up authoritative committees and commissions to study the cause of this modern scourge [lung cancer]. All have concluded that it is almost entirely due to cigarette smoking." On the other hand, the late Sir Ronald Fisher, who has been described as the greatest statistician who ever lived, feared that this conclusion would be seen in retrospect to be a "catastrophic and conspicuous howler." Where then does the truth lie? With a statistician-logician-geneticist of genius, or with "authoritative committees and commissions"?

During the past year or so I have reiterated Fisher's views in the Lancet and supplemented them with new analyses. Surprisingly, these arguments and demonstrations have provoked few responses and nothing to challenge Fisher's position. Having at one time accepted that most cases of lung cancer are caused by smoking, I shall outline in this article some of the evidence that forced me to change my mind.

For over a decade, I have been particularly interested in two kinds of phenomena: the sex- and age-dependence of natural diseases, malignant and nonmalignant, infectious and non-infectious; and the detailed anatomical distribution of the lesions of natural disease. Coupling the study of these key phenomena with other considerations led my collaborators and me to the following unexpectedly simple conclusions.

Central control of growth

Each well-defined natural disease with a reproducible age-pattern results from a specific breakdown in the central system that, in health, regulates growth and controls the size of tissues (see New Scientist, vol 40, p 404). We call such diseases autoaggressive, because one part of the body attacks another part, the target tissue. A specific autoaggressive disease is confined to persons with a specific genetic predisposition, and the age of onset is largely determined by the prior occurrence of a particular kind and number of random events—sonatic gene mutations. These mutations change the message from particular genes in stem cells of the central growth-control system. A stem cell with a full complement of specifically mutant genes divides to propagate a "forbidden clone" of descendant cells. In many diseases, more than one forbidden clone is needed for onset. Peripheral cells of the forbidden clone), or their secreted humoral products, attack complementary target cells at one or more anatomical locations to produce the symptoms and signs of disease.

Infectious and allergic diseases, we conclude, are a special form of autoaggressive disease in which an invading microorganism or allergen is essential to precipitate the growth of an initiated forbidden clone. The invading antigenic agent competes for endogenous defence (antibody) or sources that otherwise would suppress the growth of forbidden clones. An immunist person is able to cope with the double assault.

Accepting at one stage that lung cancer pre-eminently an "unnatural", carcinogenic induced disease, and being ignorant of Fisher's views, I long refrained from examining its sex- and age-dependence. However, no convincing description of the mechanism whereby smoking is supposed to cause lung cancer had emerged (it still hasn't) and eventually turned to the age-dependence of the disease in the hope that it might solve the problem. Our basic theory of autoaggressive disease enabled me to predict the types of secular change in mortality from lung cancer that should have occurred assuming various mechanisms of carcinogenesis. In principle, we have to consider that cigarette smoke might act as an initiator hastening the formation of forbidden clones and/or as a promoter, shortening the interval (latent period) between the end of initiation and the onset of lung cancer; and/or as a precipitator, causing forbidden clones to proliferate that otherwise would remain suppressed.

Mortality from lung cancer in Britain, as recorded by the Registrar General, has increased enormously during this century. So has the consumption of cigarettes. Because of these complications and the preconceptions about the cause of lung cancer, investigators have tended to follow the life history of people born in a given period (typically five or 10 years) and to determine their age-specific death-rates. This is known as cohort analysis. Happily, I decided first to cast a balance at vertical data—age-specific death-rates for a given calendar year, or a few (consecutive) years. The totally unexpected findings converted the glance to detailed scrutiny. When thoroughly explored they failed to corroborate any of the above causal models. Some representative results are shown in Figures 1 and 2.

Unexpected results

Figure 1 analyses vertical data from cancer registries for sex-specific and age-specific onset-rates in England and Wales, 1960-2. As in other countries, age-specific onset-rates for older men climb to a peak (mode) more steeply than for women and then fall sharply above about 70 years of age. Age-specific death-rates exhibit a similar behaviour because the average interval between onset and death from lung cancer is short.
