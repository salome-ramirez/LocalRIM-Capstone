RESTAURANIS AND INSTITUTIONS

THE FOODSERVICE MAGAZINE
AUGUST 1, 1997

The Drill on Customer Service

By Patricia B. Dailey R&J MANAGING EDITOR

At the end of the day, having served its customers well is the most important thing the industry can have accomplished.

Customer service—what a quaint notion, and apparently as common these days as a fin-fendered Caddy. The entire foodservice industry is predicated on providing service, yet the modern-day take on it is barely recognizable, a badly watered-down version of what it is intended to be. Cordial greetings, helpful and efficient service, and gracious thank-yous are hardly the modern-day norms, shuttled aside in favor of grunts and brusque, perfunctory, disinterested actions.

A recent survey turned up some pretty uncomfortable data. Respondents reported that they were more satisfied with the experience of facing the drill at the dentist's office than order-didn't fare all that much better—long-distance telephone operators got higher marks. Unfortunately, that is not the only finger that's been pointed; a lot of studies are landing on the same conclusions. This is not something to be proud of and certainly not a development that bodes well for the image or strength of the industry.

Ironically, there is a persistent hum about the importance of customer service, and it's a safe bet that any industry execs asked would say that they rate it very high on the list of values and mandates. Lip service is not the right response, though. It's time for meaningful action from the top of the pack.

Striking, but not at all surprising, is how the industry's success stories provide solid models. One of Starbucks' early stated goals was to provide excellent service, a critical point as it sought to sell cups of coffee that cost considerably more than many customers were accustomed to paying. The NRA's Herman Cain carved out an early victory at Burger King by instituting a "happy B.E.E." program to motivate workers to provide good service. A restaurant chain executive recently explained that, for him, it's more important to hire good, friendly people than those who are merely competent: "I can train people to do a job, but I'll never change their basic nature."

There is a proud tradition of providing service and hospitality in this industry. It is our past, but also the key to the future. Instead of everyone just blathering about how important customer service is, it's time to put those words into action. Good service isn't just one of the things we need to be concerned with. In the end, it's the only thing.
