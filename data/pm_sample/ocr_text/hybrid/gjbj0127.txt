IMPACT ANALYSES OF NEW INSTITUTIONS

For several years, under grants from NSF/RANN, The Futures Group has been conducting a series of policy analyses and impact assessments of new institutional arrangements: legalized gambling in the form of lotteries and off-track betting, prepaid legal services, and currently, unionization of state court employees. These studies have been designed to be of immediate practical use to decision makers in state and local governments, professional groups, unions, and business.

Legalized Gambling

The study of lotteries and off-track betting (The Impact of Legalized Gambling, Praeger Publishers) has been accepted as the basic text in the field. It furnishes information on the history, operations, financial returns, and economic and societal impacts of these recently spreading forms of legalized gambling, and defines policy issues for legislators and the public. It also analyzes differences in impact between existing and proposed forms of legalized gambling. The weekly lottery, for instance, is probably the least harmful form of public gambling; it is least likely to encourage heavy betting because it is a non-multiplier game (the prize is not dependent on how much one bets) and has a low bettor involvement in terms of skill and entertainment, a long payout interval, and low incentive to re-bet winnings. Casino gambling, on the other hand, has exactly opposite attributes.

Prepaid Legal Services

Prepaid legal services have recently emerged as a possible fringe benefit for workers. The Futures Group study (Prepaid Legal Services, Lexington Books) analyzes experience in the field to date, identifies potential future impacts on the legal profession, on the courts, on plan members, and on the public, and points out how impacts might differ under alternative forms of the plans. "Open" plans put no general limitation on the opportunity for any lawyer to represent members, operating in a similar manner to Blue Shield-type medical plans, while "closed" plans limit in some way the number of lawyers who may provide services, either through a selected panel or a hired staff of lawyers. Unions have generally leaned in the direction of closed plans, while bar associations in a number of states have established open plans. It appears that, assuming a rate of growth comparable to other fringe benefits, the overall impact on employment in the legal profession would not be massive, although sizable numbers could be involved (5 to 10 percent of all lawyers by 1985).
