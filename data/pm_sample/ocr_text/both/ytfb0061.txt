Activists: Ads tempt teens to smoke

Cigarette maker says aim is to deter youths

By PHUONG LY
Staff Writer

The teen, wearing side-striped jeans and leaning against a windowsill, projects a cool, confident image as she tells why she doesn't smoke.

"My parents — they always think I'm not listening," she says in the advertisement, her classmates in the background. "I hear them... I don't smoke because sometimes, it's what you don't do that makes you who you are."

The commercial flashes to other teens proudly saying they don't smoke.

The national television ad and two others launched this month by cigarette maker Philip Morris Inc. is sparking renewed attacks from anti-tobacco groups about the company's motives toward teens, a growing segment of smokers.

Philip Morris' new $100 million campaign against teen smoking is its biggest initiative on the issue and its first featuring television ads.

N.C. anti-smoking activists charge that the new ads create curiosity about smoking with their ambiguous slogan: "Think Don't Smoke."

The surgeon general's warning flashes at the bottom of the screen, but the commercials don't show the health risks associated with smoking, activists say.

"This is the fox guarding the chicken coop," said Dan Zaccaro of the N.C. Group to Alleviate Smoking Pollution (GASP), one of the state's largest anti-smoking organizations. "Why would they not want kids to start smoking? That's their future market."

The Washington-based Campaign for Tobacco-Free Kids suggested that Philip Morris quit using its controversial Marlboro Man image, rather than spending money on the new television spots.

But Philip Morris, the nation's largest cigarette maker,

said the commercials were effective in keeping kids away from an adult habit because they feature teens talking to and among their peers.

"We felt that playing a proactive role to reduce youth smoking was a responsible thing to do as corporate citizens," said spokeswoman Ellen Merlo. "We don't want kids to smoke. We market to adults."

She said the company plans additional TV as well as radio and print ads telling parents about the role they play in helping their children make smart choices.

Next year, Philip Morris plans to send brochures and teaching materials on reducing teen smoking to schools and community organizations and help groups sponsor activities for young people.

Meanwhile, Zaccaro said N.C. GASP will continue its own efforts against teen smoking. The group started a program last year that sends victims of lung cancer and other smoking-related illnesses to speak at schools.

Health advocates anticipate funds from states, including the Carolinas, for programs against teen smoking, as part of the $206 billion tobacco settlement signed last month.
