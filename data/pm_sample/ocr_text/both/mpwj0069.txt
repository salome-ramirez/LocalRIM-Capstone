Fire Safe Cigarette Bill 1999

Attachment B

CPSC’s 1993 Report to Congress

The most exhaustive analysis to date of developing a cigarette ignition propensity standard was undertaken by the Consumer Products Safety Commission (“CPSC”). In 1993, the CPSC, after six years of federal studies that included the CPSC, the National Institute of Standards and Technology (“NIST”), the Federal Trade Commission and the National Cancer Institute, reported to Congress that significant further work ought to be pursued before adoption of a cigarette ignition propensity standard. In particular, the CPSC report to Congress identified the following issues requiring resolution:

• Whether a cigarette ignition propensity standard would actually reduce the number of real-world fires caused by careless smoking is questionable.

• Whether the benefits of a standard would outweigh the costs – or vice versa – had not been determined.

• Whether the NIST tests would have any effect on reducing the number of fires caused by cigarettes.

• Whether a standard would produce cigarettes that smokers will accept had not been determined – “uncertainty about the commercial feasibility of lower ignition propensity remains.”

• Whether proposed design changes would result in an increased risk to the health of smokers. The CPSC determined that comprehensive health studies of redesigned cigarettes would be necessary and proposed the development of a multi-tier and multi-year program of chemical analyses, toxicological assays and human and animal experiments. These studies would involve significant costs and time.

Philip Morris believes that each of these issues should be considered and resolved in any process to develop cigarette ignition propensity standard.
