Small Business Briefing
Small business looks for change in labor board

Karen Kerrigan

Arbitrary labor laws that are burdensome and unfair to small businesses may get an overhaul if legislation aimed to bring fairness to National Labor Relations Board proceedings make it to President Clinton's desk.

The Congress is considering a small business labor relations board relief package of bills which would address serious problems stemming from the National Labor Relations Act and the procedures used by the labor board to enforce these laws.

Small businesses are especially vulnerable when they are hit with frivolous charges of unfair labor practices. These often arise when a business becomes the target of a union organizing drive where hardball tactics are used to financially squeeze the small employer into bowing to union demands.

The labor relations board may become the union accomplice in such cases if it discards its impartiality in such disputes. Many claim the board has abandoned its standing as neutral arbiter in labor relations, and legislation is desperately needed to restore balance and fairness to its proceedings.

"Impartiality and evenhandedness should be the NLRB's guiding principles. The current board has a decidedly partisan stance as demonstrated by its many unusual decisions and directives," said David Thompson, a workplace attorney in Washington, D.C.

Four bills moving through the Congress has garnered widespread support from those who agree that the labor relations board has allowed its procedures to be abused by unions to inflict economic harm on small businesses and their employees.

"This package will level the playing field for small businesses as they face an aggressive NLRB with vast expertise and resources," said Mr. Thompson.

The small business package includes:

The Truth in Employment Act

This legislation would remedy the unscrupulous practice of "salting."

According to the Independent Electrical Contractors, "salts" can best be described as union organizers who seek employment with open shop employers to harass or disrupt the business. The goal of salting is to drive open shops out of existence through economic hardship caused by expensive labor relations board proceedings.

The legislative measure would amend the National Labor Relations Act to make clear that an employer is not required to hire any person who seeks a job to promote interests unrelated to those of the employer. The bill would not infringe upon employee's existing protections under the act, including the right to organize.

Fair Access to Indemnity and Reimbursement

Small-business owners often cannot afford the qualified legal representation necessary to defend themselves against labor relations board charges. This act amends the National Labor Relations Act to reimburse a small business or labor organization prevailing against a labor board action the attorney's fees and expenses used to defend itself.

The act applies to any employer with 100 employees or less, and a net worth of no more than $1.4 million.

"Filing and pursuing a NLRB charge is essentially cost-free to the charging party and extremely expensive for the respondent," said Peter Rousos, director of corporate human relations for Gaylord Entertainment Corp. in Nashville, Tenn.

Mr. Rouses testified on behalf of the U.S. Chamber of Commerce before the House Committee of Education and the Workforce earlier this month.

According to Mr. Rousos, the bill would require labor board officials to be more rigorous in their investigations, "especially when it may be readily apparent that the charging party's true motive is merely coercion or harassment of the respondent."

Fair Hearing Act

Labor relations board chairman William Gould has been an outspoken proponent of the "single-site" organizing rule—a proposal that would provide unions an advantage in organizing the multunit locations of small businesses.

Mr. Gould's troubling proposal (in that he is supposed to be an arbiter of the law not an advocate of causes) would overturn a fundamental premise of the National Labor Relations Act—that the labor relations board's decisions are based on the aspects of each situation, or each separate unit.

To date, Congress has restricted board spending to prohibit Mr. Gould from implementing his new rule, but many believe a legislative fix is in order to codify the law. The legislation would stop the activist labor relations board from proceeding with the rule.

Justice on Time Act

This legislation would shorten the often long delays in the processing of cases by the labor relations board. The board would be required to issue a final decision within one year on all unfair labor practice complaints in cases where it is alleged that an employer has discharged an employee in an attempt to encourage or discourage union membership.

The median time for labor relations board processing of all unfair labor practice cases is 546 days. The goal of the bill is to ensure that employees who are entitled to reinstation will quickly get their jobs back, and employers will not be saddled with large liability payments for backpay awards.

Although the business community supports the concept of this bill, there is concern that complex cases do require longer deliberations.

These measures to protect small businesses will likely pass the Congress, but it remains questionable as to President Clinton's support for the reforms.

Karen Kerrigan is president of the Small Business Survival Committee in Washington, D.C.
