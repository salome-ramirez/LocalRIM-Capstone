"We got confirmation of an infinitesimally small amount... close to the limits of detection of our machines," Dr. Carchman says. "We felt this was not a safety issue, but the stuff didn’t smell good."

The MITC, which smells like horseradish in high concentrations, also left a metallic taste in the mouths of testers who "dry puffed" on cigarettes, or inhaled them without lighting them.

The company said continued use of the defective cigarettes could cause temporary discomfort, including coughing and wheezing. It added that pregnant women and people suffering from respiratory conditions should avoid exposure to MITC.

Oddly, the chemical was detected in the filters, but not in the plasticizer. Company researchers remain unclear as to how the MITC was produced.

As late as Thursday afternoon, another Phillip Morris official says, the company figured the odds were good that none of the contaminated cigarettes had left its plants. But by early the next morning researchers determined the presence of MITC in filters made at both the Richmond and Louisville manufacturing plants. It became clear that there was a possibility some contaminated cigarettes had reached stores.

The fact that any tainted cigarettes might have reached retailers baffles executives at Philip Morris, which prides itself on its factory quality control. The company tests each cigarette ingredient before it is used, and testers smoke samples of the final product before they are packaged and sent to distributors. "We were blown away when we found out about this," says one company executive. "We couldn’t even imagine how this could happen."

Only a fraction of the recalled cigarettes actually are defective, Philip Morris says.

The murky circumstances surrounding the incident also raise questions of whether someone could have contaminated the filters deliberately. Because plasticizer is used at all of Philip Morris’s plants and across all its brands, it was a particularly vulnerable point in the manufacturing process.

While Philip Morris executives say sabotage is unlikely, they haven’t ruled it out. Company officials said they aren’t "focused on that issue at this time."

The timing of the recall announcement—on the eve of the long Memorial Day weekend—led some industry analysts in viewwied to speculate that Philip Morris wanted to minimize negative publicity. News of the unprecedented recall, which broke a half hour before the close of trading on the New York Stock Exchange, sent Philip Morris shares down $2 to $69.875. The timing also complicated the already monumental task of getting the word out to distributors, retailers and consumers.

It's relatively easy to pull back specific cases of cigarettes as late as the distributor level, but once the packs hit store shelves, industry executives say recall logistics become infinitely more complicated. While Philip Morris can reach about 70% of its sales volume via large chains and retailers, the remainder is sold through tiny and far-flung stores.

Confusion seemed to be the rule in New York City over the weekend. Late Sunday night, a number of independent delicatessens professed no knowledge of the recall and continued to sell Marlboro and other Philip Morris brands as usual. At a newsstand at Pennsylvania Station, a clerk refused to provide a refund without a receipt showing that the cigarettes had been purchased there.

"This isn’t a pushbutton process," says a Philip Morris spokesman, who adds the company expects to have reached retailers representing more than 50% of its sales by today and will finish contacting the rest by the end of this week.

In the past year, Philip Morris’s Marlboro brand has racked up impressive market-share and sales gains. Now, with the market leader out of many stores for a few days, Philip Morris’s rivals have the nation’s smokers entirely to themselves.

Indeed, executives close to R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. say the company was monitoring its inventories over the weekend, and is already getting calls from distributors asking for more product. "It’s a free-sampling chance to get some packs in their hands," says a person close to RJR. Smokers switching to a new brand "might like it and stay with it." Reynolds says it hasn’t used plasticizer from Hoechst in over a year.

A Philip Morris spokesman said there shouldn't be any supply disruptions at many stores.

Smokers, meanwhile, didn’t show much concern about this latest — and apparently temporary — problem connected with their habit. A tobacco retailer in Massapequa on New York’s Long Island says that about half his cigarette customers said they didn’t care about the recall and went ahead and purchased Philip Morris brands anyway.
