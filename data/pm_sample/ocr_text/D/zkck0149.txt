Recent emphasis on high resolution gradient echo studies in functional imaging has led to the conclusion that there are likely three domains of response to the blood circulation in the brain when considering field inhomogeneity effects of the venous blood pre- and during activation. The first is a coherent effect due to large or macroscopic vessels on the order of the size of the voxel (ca 200–500 µm in most studies). These can lead to very large signal changes (up to 100%). The second is at the venule level (ca 50–200 µm) and is associated with smaller parenchymal changes (usually ca 10% or less). The third is at the capillary level and is associated with much smaller signal changes at 1.5 T and even up to 4 T. The actual signal changes depend on field strength and sequence design. In this paper, we present our experience in detecting the first two domains with 2D and 3D gradient echo imaging at 1.5 T. We find that high resolution enables visualization of the larger small veins in motor cortex studies and that, on occasion, it is possible to see the venule effects as well. We suggest a simple model to explain the large signal changes based on susceptibility changes and partial volume effects. Comparisons of the functional imaging data to this model and to MR angiographic studies are also shown as evidence of the venous sources of the susceptibility changes.

INTRODUCTION

Functional neuroimaging and its application to the study of human mental processes (both in normal and diseased states) is acknowledged as one of the most fundamental and challenging aspects of current neuroscience research. Until relatively recently, this research has relied predominantly on positron emission tomography and its associated radionuclide studies and on single photon emission computed tomography. These modalities have indeed yielded valuable information on mental functioning but are constrained by technological shortcomings which include relatively poor temporal (one image per minute) and spatial (50–100 mm³ voxel size) resolution. In addition, their reliance upon radioligands, while offering the distinct advantage of being able to image a large group of chemical substances, also limits the general practicality of these modalities. Multiple detector electroencephalographic and magnetoencephalographic systems are now becoming available for rapid temporal resolution and, when registered with structural MRI, offer excellent opportunities for functional neuroimaging. These EEG systems can achieve millisecond sampling but suffer from the ill-posed nature of the problem since the local tissue conductivities are not known (although MR may help overcome this problem).

MR perfusion/diffusion have been used with contrast agents to predict cerebral blood flow using gradient echo and echo-planar techniques. These dynamic techniques are fast enough to allow tracer methods to be applied. Although $T_1$ changes were initially the focus of contrast agents, $T_2$ agents such as dysprosium were also evaluated. Several papers clearly showed the dependence of the signal changes with oxygenation as a function of the susceptibility. Flow itself has also been used to measure functional imaging changes. These changes are usually quite small in signal intensity. The small signal changes have generally required special processing methods when multiple 2D scans have been acquired.

Our efforts have been directed toward understanding the sources of the signal changes using gradient echo imaging. Stemming from the use of these sequences, arguments have appeared that brain parenchymal changes are easily seen at 1.5 T and that percentage changes can be quite large. We proposed that such changes could only be due to larger vessels (i.e., those on the order of the voxel size). The mechanism suggested was a simple two-component model that led to dramatic changes in signal as the voxel size decreased.
