Overview
A recent Wall Street Journal/NBC News survey found that the loss of personal privacy is the issue that concerns Americans most about the new millennium. Loss of privacy (29 percent) causes more concern than terrorism and overpopulation (both 23 percent), world war (16 percent), and global warming (14 percent).

Future worries aside, a review of recent media coverage shows the privacy issue today to be complex, heavily publicized, a hotbed for activists, and a growing topic of consumer awareness. Gathering and selling personal information is not new, so why so many red flags now? Easier, cheaper technologies have opened the floodgates to a huge amount of personal information that is now readily available to both government and businesses.

How is the public reacting to business and government peering more and more into their lives? According to recent consumer surveys, in the marketing realm consumers generally seem to be treating privacy as an “asset” that they can choose to trade, barter, or sell.

In other areas—insurance, public health, and employment—new conflicts are emerging between personal privacy interests and information organizations. All of this is leading to a growing body of federal and state law that tries to strike a better balance between the two.

For corporate affairs professionals charged with helping our businesses achieve their objectives, there are both potential pitfalls and opportunities present in today's privacy arena. Examples of both, as well as questions for us to consider, are set forth in the following pages.
