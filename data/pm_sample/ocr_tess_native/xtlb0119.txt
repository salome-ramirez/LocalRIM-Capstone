Three hundred fifteen smokers who attended a family practice clinic and wished
to quit smoking were assigned in a random, double-blind manner to receive
either nicotine (2 mg) or placebo gum. Smokers initially received brief advice
from a physician and nurse, a slide presentation and written materials (29 to 35
minutes), and a single follow-up visit (12 to 20 minutes) one week after cessa-
tion. After corrections for marital status and income, 10% of those who received
nicotine gum and 7% of those who received placebo gum reported continuous
abstinence for 11 months and passed observer and biochemical! verification
{this difference was not statistically significant). We conciude that, when used in
& nonselected group of smokers along with a brief intervention in a general
medical practice, the pharmacologic effects of nicotine gum to increase cassa-
tion are either small or nonexistent.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS
Subjects
Three hundred fifteen smokers were
recruited from two nonacademic, family
practice clinics of a health maintenance
organization. The clinies were located in
suburban Minneapolis. Subjects were
recruited both by physician referral and
by. word of mouth. Recruitment oc
curred between November 1984 and
June 1985, which was nine to 16 months
after nicotine gum began being mar-
keted in the United States.
Inclusion criteria were daily cigarette
smoking, a desire to stop smoking (as
indicated by willingness to set a quit
date within two weeks), and the ability
to chew gum. Exclusion criteria were
prior use of nicotine gum, use of tobacco
