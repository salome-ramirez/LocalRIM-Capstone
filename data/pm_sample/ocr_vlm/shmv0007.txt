A 25/25 VISION FOR DINERS EVERYWHERE

You’d think someone had uncovered Machu Picchu’s northern annex under Gramercy Park, the way New Yorkers are suddenly scurrying to Park Avenue South. What happened? Some good restaurants opened within a few blocks of each other. That’s it. But the press has proclaimed, “Land not!” and rescued this forgotten neighborhood from years of object obscurity.

Hmmm. Twenty-five years ago, when this magazine started, everyone liked to hang, too. Except everyone liked to hang out where Andy hung out. And Ancy hung out at Max’s Kansas City. At Park Avenue South and 17th Street; to be exact. So much for declarations of discovery. I’d rather praise consistency.

25 DINING PLEASURES

5 stress-reducers for a special evening
• Go somewhere where they know you and treat you like a relative who’s never borrowed anything, or where you can drop a reference. Anyone who goes to a place that’s been open less than three months—even, or especially, if it’s just been reviewed—serves whatever they get.
• Confirm your reservation the afternoon of your dinner. Get the name of the person you spoke with. If the place takes no reservations (a ridiculous practice for any establishment charging more than nine dollars for a bowl of pasta), make a backup one somewhere else (but cancel if you don’t use it).
• Put the time in your agenda fifteen minutes earlier than it really is. You’ll know it, but you won’t. It works.
• Try on what you’re going to wear the night before. Pants may suddenly be too tight. A moth may have eaten before you. You may have forgotten you no longer have shoes to match, or that your shirt scratches against your most recent body piercing.
• Clip your nose nails the night before.

5 things to do once you’re seated
• Have the waiter explain absolutely everything on the menu you don’t understand and are interested in. Ask if there are any items not on the menu that night. Of course, you shouldn’t have to, but you shouldn’t have to afraid on the subway, and Yanni shouldn’t have platinum records. It happens.
• Take your shoes off.
• Trust the chef to do things his way, at least once. Otherwise, why bother eating his food at all?
• Forget about what mom did to liver, turnips, and beets. So what if you don’t know what quinoa, quince, morels, shad, or trifle taste like? Try something you’ve never had.
• Learn to accept a compliment with grace. It helps if there’s something stuck between your teeth when you smile and say thank you.

5 meal enhancements
• Don’t schedule a meal as a prelude to anything else—the movies, a concert, whatever. Let it run its course.
• Wear loose clothing, preferably not what you wore to work.
• Try not shoveling it in. In case you’d gotten, you’re not simply refueling.
• Avoid talking about business, money, things you must get done tomorrow, and any opening segment of Hard Copy.
• Go see Eat Drink Man Woman or rent Babette’s Feast first.

5 tenets of good dinner conversation
• Don’t be afraid of silence.
• If you can’t help it, start with a margarita.
• Talk about what’s in front of you. At least that’s one experience you’re sharing.
• Read the paper that day. Or at least watch Entertainment Tonight.
• Don’t bring up The Celestine Prophecy.

5 niceties people will remember
• You stand whenever a woman approaches or leaves your table.
• You know something about wine but don’t snow off by picking the most expensive one just because you’re all splitting it.
• You can’t smoke while others are eating. You reapply your makeup in the bathroom.
• You don’t loosen your belt and say things like “I’m stuffed.”
• You go to the cash machine before dinner.

25 WHININGS IN DINING

5 things you don’t do in a restaurant
• Break up.
• Lay your keys on the table.
• Tell the waiter everything is O.K. when it’s not. Think who you’re really inconveniencing. Now think who’s paying for it.
• Snap your fingers for attention, unless you’re headlining the floor show in a Spanish restaurant and wearing one of those great dresses with the train and all those ruffles.
• Touch the waiter.

5 wardrobe elements that make no sense in a restaurant
• Suede. Not even shoes.
• A dress that has all its best detailing below the waist.
• Ruffles. Tight shoes. Your feet are going to swell.
• A tie—unless you feel like it.

5 really dumb things to say at dinner
• To the waiter: “Is it good? What if I don’t like it?”
• To the bartender: “I’ll have a screwdriver with Stoli.”
• To a person at the next table: “Sorry, but can I burn a Merit?”
• To the sommelier: “Nah, just pour. I’m sure it’s fine.”
• To your date: “I really like you, as a friend.”

5 reasons why I’d rather swallow raw tripe than eat in a public place with you again
• You start before everyone else gets served, then don’t share.
• You don’t look the waiter in the eye when ordering and then you grab the busboy by the arm while speaking to him in broken English.
• You sit in a three-star restaurant and all you can talk about is business.
• You tip by doubling the tax exactly, regardless of the quality of service.
• You gesture with silverware.
