Burned up over smoking

The New York State Public Health Council is in over its head. It has just given preliminary okay to a set of rules on smoking that are among the harshest in the nation. Lighting up would be verboten in most public areas. Cars, private homes and large pastures would be among the few places left in the state where smoking is permitted.

Health Commissioner David Axelrod says the council's rules are justified "in the communal interest." A powerful point. Smoking is unhealthy, both for the puffer and those who breathe what he or she exhales.

But good intentions don't justify the council's grabbing for power beyond its reach. Only the Legislature, made up of elected representatives of the people, has the authority to enact such sweeping changes in the law.

The courts almost certainly will overturn the rules on precisely this point—the decision must be made by legislators. What's the use of the council's marching up the no-smoking hill only to be knocked back down again? That's setting back the very cause it professes to support.

Smoke in their eyes

With problems like the crack epidemic, AIDS and teenage pregnancy on its plate, the state Public Health Council should have its hands full.

Surprisingly, this doesn't seem to be the case — judging by the extraordinary amount of time and energy it is devoting to harrassing smokers.

The Health Council — an un-elected body composed of 14 gubernatorial appointees — has plans to promulgate the strictest anti-smoking regulations in the country.

Unless the state Legislature acts to stop it, the council — at the end of March — will ban smoking in virtually all public places.

For practical purposes, smoking will be allowed only in private homes, privately sponsored social events, bars, hotel rooms and, of course, tobacco stores.

The council's obsession with smoking reflects the zealotry of state Health Commissioner Dr. David Axelrod who — though a bit slow on closing homosexual bathhouses — describes the anti-smoking regulations as a matter of "communal interest."

The most unhealthy aspect of this enterprise is the fact that Axelrod and the council are trying to promulgate by edict laws that have actually been rejected, at least three times, by the state Legislature.

There is, to be sure, a civil rights issue here — it turns on the intrusion by an unelected "council" into an area of human relations best regulated by common courtesy, not legal penalties.

What's next? A ban on alcohol? (whoops, that's been tried before.) Legislators — take heed.
