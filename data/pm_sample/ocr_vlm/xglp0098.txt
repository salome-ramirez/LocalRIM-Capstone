FOUNDATION MESSAGE

Of Surpluses, Tax Hikes, And Tobacco

Washington has seen some pretty strange sights in recent years, and so it really takes an effort to surprise. But President Clinton has come through again. His own Treasury Department projects a cumulative surplus of $714 billion over the next five years and yet the President proposes a net tax hike of $45 billion in his budget.

To put his tax increase in context, the Office of Management and Budget projects federal taxes to reach 20.7 percent of GDP in 1999, a modern record. Only at the very peak of World War II spending in 1944 did this ratio reach a higher level of 20.9 percent. Even more relevant is the tax burden per capita. The Tax Foundation estimated the federal per capita tax burden to be $6,810 in 1998 with a sizable increase expected in 1999. When President Clinton took office, the per capita federal tax burden was $5,441 when adjusted for inflation. That’s a $1,368 increase, or over 25 percent.

Curiously missing from the President’s voluminous budget submission is the distribution of the President’s net tax hike by income class. Tax Foundation senior economist Patrick Fleenor rectified the omission by publishing it in Number 84 of the Foundation’s Special Report series, The President’s Fiscal Year 2000 Budget. The table displays the President’s second surprise — his plan is highly regressive. Taxpayers with incomes below $50,000 in adjusted gross income would pay over 60 percent of the net tax increase, and taxpayers making less than $25,000 will pay the majority of that, over 38 percent.

The plan is so regressive because its centerpiece is a proposed 55-cent-per-pack increase in the tobacco excise. Recall last year the tobacco companies agreed to pay the states hundreds of billions of dollars, supposedly compensating them for costs incurred in their public health programs due to smoking. Naturally, the fed tried to lay claim to that pool of cash, but the federal deal fell through, and the states wisely cut out the middle man, Uncle Sam, and came to a settlement on their own. The states haven’t decided yet how to divvy up the $195 billion payoff, but they’ll never let the feds in on the split.

The settlement’s justification was that smokers cost society extra. A recent study by Jane Gravelle of the Congressional Research Institute and published by the Tax Foundation notes that smokers raised Medicare and Medicaid’s health care costs by about $9 billion annually. She also notes, however, that because smokers tend to die younger they reduce Social Security outlays by about $40 billion and reduce nursing home costs borne by Medicare by about $8 billion. Factor in cigarette tax revenue, and Gravelle concludes that the federal government saves almost $35 billion each year due to smoking and the states save $2.1 billion. These estimates don’t warrant subsidizing cigarettes, but they cast grave doubt on the fiscal excuses offered up to justify federal and state cigarette excises.

That hasn’t stopped the states who are clearly not satisfied with their extraordinary haul from the tobacco companies. Even knowing that their poorest citizens will bear the load of any new taxes on cigarettes, five state governments raised their cigarette tax rates in 1998: Hawaii from 80 cents per pack to $1.00 (tieing Alaska for the highest rate), New Jersey from 40 to 80 cents, Illinois from 44 to 58 cents, Pennsylvania from 61 to 71 cents, and Wisconsin from 44 to 59 cents.

Governor Glendenning of Maryland has proposed putting his state at the top of the cigarette tax charts with a $1-per-pack hike on top of the existing 36-cent state tax. As a rationalization, he has resorted to another popular argument for higher cigarette excises, saying that this would discourage underage smoking. The governor should not mock such a serious problem with a proposal designed to fail.

Only two percent of cigarettes are smoked by the underage, yet he would have all Maryland smokers—a mostly low-income group—pay more tax because he is unwilling to enforce laws already on the books against underage smoking. The state could ban cigarettes from vending machines or crack down on retailers, but those tactics wouldn’t put more money in the state treasury.

According to the University of Michigan’s Monitoring the Future Survey, while 36.5 percent of high school seniors smoked at least one cigarette in the prior month, only 24.6 percent smoked at least one a day, and only 14.3 percent smoked more than ten cigarettes daily. For a teenager smoking ten a day, a $1 per pack tax hike amounts to $3.50 a week extra cost. The effect of the price hike is likely to be blunted by the fact, observed by Gravelle, that teenagers tend to buy higher priced premium brands because they smoke for status. Status smokers are more likely to be occasional smokers. Thus they can afford premium brands and, in all likelihood, they will pay the taxes with virtually no change in their behavior.

However, some people will be forced to change. A low-income couple that smokes a pack a day each will be facing at least $750 more in taxes each year. Will they give up cigarettes or necessities? You be the judge.

When examined closely, virtually all fiscal arguments in favor of imposing any tobacco excise taxes, let alone higher taxes, simply fall. The only arguments left standing are that smoking is unpopular in important quarters and governments need only a thin excuse to raise a tax.

Even so, it takes real audacity for President Clinton to suggest that a government in surplus, already imposing the heaviest peacetime tax burden in history, needs more revenue from lower-income taxpayers. Perhaps that is the one legacy of the Administration on which we can all agree—it never lacked audacity.
