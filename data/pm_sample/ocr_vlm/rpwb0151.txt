The Editor, Cancer Research

Dear Sir,

Pauly makes a huge and probably erroneous assumption in his recent article (1) when he says that the two fibers he found in human lungs are cellulose acetate (CA) fibers from a cigarette filter.

Since the early-1950's, most American cigarettes have incorporated filters. Typically, fibrous material, which is drawn into a nonwoven web cut to lengths of 10-30 mm and diameters of 7.5-8.5 mm, is placed on the mouth end of the cigarette. A number of filter materials have been used to reduce the cigarette yield of "tar" (and other smoke constituents) in the smoker. By far the most common filter material in use today is CA: an odorless, tasteless, nontoxic solid material (2). CA fibers used in cigarette filters have Y-shaped cross-sections, with the diameter of the circle circumscribing the Y varying from 20 to 60 µm (2). The way the CA fibers are integrated into the filter, the crimp factor that is imparted during manufacture, and the addition of a plasticizer are all design characteristics that would tend to eliminate fiber "leakage" (2).

The "evidence" supposedly proving that fibers found in lung samples are from a cigarette filter is as follows: (a), the fibers have the same Y-shaped cross-section (formed by liquid CA being forced through triangular jets, the triangular cross-section being gradually transformed into the Y by the inward collapse of the cross-section as solvent is lost during drying), (b), the fibers can be made to fluoresce (as do filter fibers from cigarettes that have been smoked), and (c), the fibers contain small amounts of titanium dioxide (as do fibers from cigarette filters) (2). This evidence is circumstantial at best. Y-shaped fibers are not necessarily CA fibers (3). CA fibers are manufactured in a wide range of shapes and sizes and are used in a large number of consumer applications, including "lingerle, dresses, blouses, robes and other apparel, linings, draperies, bedspreads, upholstery, curpets, umbrellas, formed fabrics, and cigarette filters" (3). Many of these myriad uses of CA fibers require the addition of both titanium dioxide (used as a delusterant, since CA is naturally lustrous), and a range of dyes, many of which would be fluorescent. Fibers with a Y cross-section, which fluoresce, and which contain titanium dioxide, are simply not unique to cigarette filters. The entire article is based on the simple assumption that the fiber(s) they found in human lungs are from cigarette filters. There is in fact no real justification for the assumption, since there are any number of sources of fibers that meet the authors' criteria.
